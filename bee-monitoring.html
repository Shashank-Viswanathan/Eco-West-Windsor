The Future of Monitoring West Windsor’s Bees

The author of this article, Elyse Talley, is a PhD candidate in the Winfree Lab in the Department of Ecology, Evolution, and Natural Resources at Rutgers University. Working under Dr. Rachael Winfree in her lab, Talley’s research thesis examines the influence of habitat patch properties on wild bee and plant biodiversity through her work with the National Park Service, Boston Harbor Islands.

Why We Need to Monitor Bees at All

Whereas one might envision honeybees as managed hives in a typical depiction of pollinators, there are native wild bees, from bumble bees to shiny solitary bees, that do the real work in suburban areas such as West Windsor, NJ. In order to protect these wild populations of bees, it is crucial that we understand what species are found in our towns, when they appear, and how the populations change over time.

But, monitoring wild bee populations poses an unusual ecological challenge. As Rutgers graduate student Elyse Talley points out, "From the perspective of wanting to conserve bees, we obviously need to be able to study them and identify them... most bees you can't identify unless you put them under a microscope, which is why we tend to use lethal sampling."

Lethal Sampling and Its Bottlenecks

Ecologists usually employ methods of lethal trapping such as the use of pan traps (colorful plastic bowls containing soapy water) or blue vane traps in order to collect physical specimens for identification in the laboratory. Although these methods can be useful for collecting physical specimens for microscopy, they soon become unwieldy in their scale.

As Talley explains in regards to her dissertation research, "For my project, I used pan traps and I caught 8,000 bees."

Capturing thousands of insects will cause an enormous delay in scientific studies. Based on the time it takes to analyze physical information, Talley asserts that "with lethal sampling, I would definitely say [there's a] huge bottleneck in the time it takes to process all the specimens. You often get way more specimens than you actually can handle, or that you actually would need to answer your questions."

The problem is exacerbated by the lack of taxonomic specialists across the country capable of identifying the exact species. Talley observes this issue, saying that "there tend to be very few taxonomists that actually can ID most bee species... all the different bee labs across the country are sending their specimens off to a couple of taxonomists, and you have to wait months usually to get your specimens ID'd."

Beyond the logistical costs, traditional sampling can introduce data bias. As Talley points out, "Some bee species are really, really attracted to pan traps and vane traps... even though they're there and locally abundant, they would never fly into a pan trap. And some bees tend to go into pan traps in really high abundances. We don't really know why that is."

Camera Traps and Computer Vision

In order to overcome problems associated with lethal sampling, scientists are turning to non-lethal options: the use of camera traps that react to movement and computer software for analyzing images.

The transition from using physical traps is obvious in terms of ethics and conservation. As Talley states, "The major advantage is that you're not killing thousands of bees."

Nevertheless, using digital pictures in place of pin boards creates new challenges in data collection, such as pseudoreplication, which is the "same bee twice" phenomenon. As he explains how one insect can exaggerate population numbers, Talley shows "if a bee flies into the trap, you take a picture of it, it flies out. It could just fly right back in … you take another picture of it, and you think it's a different bee, and it's not, it's the same individual."

Where the AI Model Remains a Hurdle

It takes AI technology to comb through thousands of images taken by cameras in an automatic manner. As Talley points out, "You have to have an AI system that would look at each of the pictures and identify what the species is. And again, that's the part that isn't really perfected either … from a lot of the photos, you couldn't really tell what the species is. You could go to genus level, but not species usually."

In the evaluation of recent developments in AI technologies, such as Dr. Brian Spiesman's BeeMachine classifier system, Talley shows that "I think the last I checked, those models were at 90% accuracy in terms of being able to identify bee species. Unless we're talking about bumblebees. Bumblebees are way easier to identify to species."

While going from big and conspicuous bumblebees to small and inconspicuous bees, it is harder to identify the species. As Talley points out, "to genus level, it's pretty accurate right now as is, but obviously it depends on training your model on a ton of images. It just takes time to do that."

Also, maintaining digital camera nodes continuously involves additional costs behind the scenes. As Talley stresses, "You need a lot of data storage to be able to have all of these photos... you could have someone go through them using their eyes, but that would be an insane amount of work."

What This Means for West Windsor Gardens

As computer vision is under study in academia, knowledge of bee biology provides practical implications for locals who wish to enhance their suburban flowers in West Windsor.

In order to create backyard areas for pollinators, Talley recommends that "larger patches of habitat tend to support more bee diversity... you want to maximize the floral diversity that you have inside of the garden. You want to be planting things that are blooming continuously throughout the year."

Since various wild bees become active at different times in spring and summer, Talley proposes that "every bee tends to have preferences, every bee flies at different times. So if you want to maximize the amount of diversity that you're supporting, you want to be considering all of those things."

Busting one myth held by homeowners, Talley makes it clear that "bees definitely use non-native plants. That's a common misconception, I would say."

Apart from gathering pollen from the flowers, bees need proper nesting sites. According to Talley, "a lot of bees nest in the ground, so having patches of bare ground can often be helpful... [bee hotels] can get tricky sometimes. You have to clean them every year, or you might be spreading disease in the bee population if your bee hotels become infected."

Looking Ahead

Non-invasive automated monitoring is an interesting path into the future of field ecology. In terms of where the research is at the moment, Talley states that "we have not actually fully developed or tested any of those methods yet. Basically that's just something that my advisor is interested in developing in the future, specifically to look at how bees respond to climate change and heatwaves... It's something that will probably start up in the next couple of years."

Knowing the strengths and weaknesses of new monitoring tools allows the community projects to know the way forward. Through integrating neighborhood restoration efforts and inexpensive digital technologies, platforms such as Eco West Windsor provide a peek into what future non-invasive community-based pollinator research could look like.
